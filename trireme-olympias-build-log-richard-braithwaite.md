## Post 1 — POST 1 - BACKGROUND AND ORIGINAL OBJECTIVES  (May 16 May 16)


May 16 May 16

In 2006 I made a rowing machine model of a simple monoreme galley and then went on to design a more sophisticated machine that could drive a trireme model. I built a 1:24 section of John Coates Trireme to prove to myself that I could fit my machinery in a scale model and then embarked on this project as a challenge to see if I could make it work. I wrote to John Coates at the time and discussed a number of trials that we might be able to do with the model to add to the information obtained from the full size trials, got hold of some drawings from him and away I went.

Since then the project grew and grew, from a relatively simple test model subject to a detailed test of my own craftsmanship (and timescales have grown accordingly...). Its this aspect of the model that I have found most interesting and enjoyable and so will probably not go ahead and fit rowing machinery to this model in the end. I think Ill probably build a really simple foam model for actual wet testing and, maybe, mechanised rowing, who knows.

Having said that I have meticulously weighed everything along the way, and was amazed to find that when I weighed the model recently it was within 2% of the scaled equivalent to the lightship weight of the full size ship. That's a much better result than I was able to get at work through weight control during full size ship builds. So, I've either been incredibly accurate at following the drawings or my various errors have cancelled out and (coincidentally) ended up equal to the errors made in building the full size ship!

However, given that it seems to be a quite accurate model in terms of weight distribution, I am planning to use it to measure radius of gyration to inform another project I have looking at oared warship performance - so it does have some remaining "scientific" relevance!

Anyway, the rest of this log is about model building for the sake of model building. Although I will do another topic on rowing machines for those who are interested.

## Post 2 — POST 2 HISTORICAL BACKGROUND  (May 17 May 17)


May 17 May 17

The development of ramming as the primary tactic for ship to ship combat in the 5th Cent BC required the optimisation of the naval platform as a high speed guided missile culminating with the appearance of the Athenian Trireme which was probably the fastest oar powered warship of all time. No other warship platform has ever been so uncompromisingly designed as a weapon system in its own right since. Later Roman and Venetian galleys were "simply" platforms for other weapon systems such as troops, catapult's and cannon, and warship design has remained pretty much this way ever since.

The impact of these vessels on history should, also, not be underestimated.

In 480 BC the Athenians built a fleet of 200 Triremes and decisively defeated the Persian fleet at the Battle of Salamis, effectively ending the second Persian invasion of Greece . It has been argued that making Greece safe from invasion, provided the stability necessary for the development of much of the philosophy, ideas of democracy, science and personal freedom on which Western Civilisation has been based.

The critical importance of the Trireme to the Athenians continued after Salamis. The Greek States divided into two major alliance's. The Peloponnesian League, dominated by the Spartans (who excelled in land warfare) and the Delian League (dominated by the Naval power of Athens). The Delian League was originally formed in 478BC as a defensive alliance against the Persians but morphed into an effective Athenian Empire by the middle of the century.

![Picture1.png](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_05/Picture1.thumb.png.c8d339b2ba6f13e1c7440f802764ce0b.png)

War between these two alliances (The Peloponesian War) broke out in 431 BC. Athens protected itself from the Spartan land based armies by fortifying the town and connecting these fortifications to a fortified port at Pireus by the long walls. At Pireus the Athenians maintained a large fleet of Triremes that enabled them to dominate the Aegean and resupply Athens from their maritime empire and so resist any attempt at siege.

![Picture2.jpg](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_05/Picture2.jpg.ca947cf451a68eaf0c28803c7dc23eee.jpg)

By maintaining this fleet and the highly trained crews required, the Athenians were able to maintain this naval dominance until they lost their fleet in an ill conceived attempt to invade Sicily in 415 BC, an adventure from which they never really recovered. This period (from the defeat of the Persians in 480 BC at Salamis) is often referred to as the Golden Age of Athens and was enabled to a very significant extent by the the Athenian Trireme.

## Post 3 — POST 3 THE "OLYMPIAS" RECONSTRUCTION  (May 17 May 17)


May 17 May 17

It is surprising, given how important these vessels were to them and the extensive evidence they left behind about the rest of their lives, is how little there is about the trireme design in their literature. The historian Thucydides served as a Trierarch for a time (as all wealthy male Athenians were required to do) and we have some idea about how they were operated and their performance from his writings, but the actual design is a mystery.

Unlike merchant vessels which were heavily laden and occasionally sank to be preserved on the sea bottom, the trireme floated after it was holed in combat or wrecked. The victor would tow them away if they could or they would be left to quickly rot on the surface and disappear.

However, we do know how big they were from the excavation of the ship sheds in Pireus and we know how many oarsmen there were on board (Thucydides).

Other evidence, such as the Lenormant relief, pottery shards etc. is open to interpretation.

It is generally accepted that there were 170 oarsmen on board but one of the great controversies is around the number of oars. Was it 3 men per oar on one level like the later Venetian Triremes of the Renaissance, or did each of the 170 man crew wield his own oar on 3 levels (as suggested by Thucydides and the Athenian Naval Inventories)?

If at 3 levels did upper oars need to be longer which would have made it difficult to keep time.

Morrisson, a classics professor at Cambridge proposed an arrangement that, he argued, could work with three levels of equal length in his paper published in 1941.

![Controversy.jpg](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_05/Controversy.thumb.jpg.b6dc91db7aad1b41d01fb639f440e85b.jpg)

Each man operating his own oar has the potential to be more efficient enabling greater acceleration and manoeuvrability, but requires a highly trained crew.

The later Venetian triremes and quadremes required less well trained crews (i.e. one decent rower per oar). However, agility not so important when the galleys were essentially platforms for marines and artillery.

In the early 1980’s Morisson teamed up with an MOD Naval Architect (John Coates) to develop a design reconstruction to demonstrate the feasibility of a three tiered vessel that complied with the evidence available.

![MorrisonCoates.png](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_05/MorrisonCoates.png.3831ea9aee2fcdef3b053f10d9634d1d.png)

John believed that, when you consider all the evidence that there is, together with the properties of the materials available, you are actually so constrained that there are not actually that many ways you can make it work.

The project went through a number of phases (including a section that was taken to boat shows and Heley Royal Regatta) culminating in the construction of a full size reconstruction packing 170 oarsmen into a 37m long hull.

The resulting vessel was partially funded by the Greek Government and was commissioned into their Navy.

She is listed as a warship in the Hellenic Navy in Janes fighting ships, which is a nice touch.

The Navy allowed the Trireme Trust to conduct a number of trials which, despite the prototype nature of the design and the short periods available to train crews of 170 diverse oarsmen and women, clearly demonstrated that the arrangement was feasible and that the sort of speeds and endurance stated in texts such as Thucydides would be possible.

It was a remarkable exercise in experimental archaeology and the rigor that the MoD Chief Naval Architect and a Classics professor brought to the project means that the data collected was of a particularly high quality. Speed trials were conducted (max speed recorded 8.9 kts, turning trials in different configurations of oarsmen port and starboard achieved a turning circle of 2.5 boat lengths.

The ship is now laid up in the Greek Naval Dockyard in Pireus and can apparently be visited.

John Coates ship was effectively a prototype, unlike the Athenian Trireme that had been developed and refined over hundreds of years, so I think it is quite remarkable that it worked so well. There were, of course, many aspects that didn’t work so well and lots of potential for development of the design. John did develop proposals for a mark 2 design that addressed some of these.

This project is an attempt to build as accurate a model of this reconstruction as I can.

## Post 4 — POST 4 SOURCES  (May 19 May 19)


May 19 May 19

Olympias is a particularly well documented subject for a model build.

The actual ship still exists (as of 2024). She is, in theory, a ship in the Helenic Navy and features in Janes Fighting Ships. She is currently out of the water under cover in Piraeus Shipyard Athens. It is worth noting, however, that Olympias has been extensively modified throughout her life (as you would expect from an experimental reconstruction). Many of the earlier modifications were design changes aimed at improving the operability of the vessel, however a lot of what you see on the vessel today are repairs and strengthening works aimed at prolonging the vessels operational life.

## Post 5 — POST 5 Materials  (May 21 May 21)


May 21 May 21

Olympias was built from Douglas fir which has a density of around 530 kg/m3. Part of the purpose of this project was to produce a model with similar weight characteristics (total weight and distribution) so that data could be extracted to support software simulations (e.g. radius of gyration) a wood with a similar density was required.

Lime was chosen for the bulk of the construction as it has a similar density (also around 530 kg/m3) and also a very fine grain which I thought would look right at 1:24 scale.

Holly was chosen for some of the finer details, and where a harder wood was required as it is much harder than Lime and also has a very fine wood grain (a little like a scale version of white oak).

All wood joints were glued with epoxy resin to guarantee water resistance.

## Post 6 — POST 6 JIG AND KEEL  (May 21 May 21)


May 21 May 21

Sections on the John Coates lines plan (Plan 7) was traced (from a tif file) in AutoCAD and then spaced out to make up a 3D lines plan.

![Screenshot 2026-05-21 150802.png](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_05/Screenshot2026-05-21150802.png.3bf5ac044556f85cc586aedef541c744.png)

Extracts from Plans 7 and 13 ( © Estate of John F. Coates, reproduced with permission)

Splines were then fixed to these sections in AutoCAD to define the plank seams, using Plan 13 as a guide (after a bit of fairing). I then printed out the sections and fixed them to 1/8 “ ply. The chequered ruler at the top of each section was so that I could adjust the plot to print accurately at the right scale.

These section moulds were then glued to an MDF base plate mounted on a pine frame.

The jig was designed to be flexible so the hull could be inverted and worked on from the underside or inside, depending on what made sense.

The ribands fitted were to hold the section moulds accurately in the correct plane and were removed as the planking progressed.

I have since discovered that the Trireme Trust has a table of offsets for the hull (see POST 4) and I would use that, if I was doing this again to generate a more accurate hullform.

## Post 7 — POST 7 PLANKING AND FRAMING HULL (1)  (May 21 May 21)


May 21 May 21

The construction method used for Olympias allowed a much longer vessel to be constructed than would have been possible using the later plank on frame methodology where hogging was always a problem. On the full size Olympias the planks were mortised into one another to stop them sliding over one another and so resisting shear deflection (hogging).

![Picture2.png](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_05/Picture2.png.f8fbe01ea2033ab771c8566f86f3aec6.png)

In the case of the model these mortices are omitted and the planks are simply glued together along their edges.

The hull was planked, in the same manner as the full size ship, in 3 stages as shown below:

![Hull Build.jpg](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_05/HullBuild.thumb.jpg.6232577241d5f0c25f2f94ece7a6be8c.jpg)

Extract from Plan 8( © Estate of John F. Coates, reproduced with permission)

Each plank was bent over but not attached to the frame templates and glued edge of the previous plank. At the end of each phase the relevant framing was shaped to fit the inside of the planking and glued into place before “fastening” through the planking with wooden pegs to simulate the bolting used to fix the planks to the frames in the full size ship.

![P7161545.JPG](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_05/P7161545.thumb.JPG.f5055dd2418d4e718a7cf1ba3ddfc6bd.JPG)

The first plank (garboard) to be fitted was made of 3 lengths butts positioned as shown in Plan No 14) glued into the keel rabet and joggled into the keel fore and aft. The plank buts were square scarf joints The next plank was glued to the garboard plank and also joggled into the keel at the fore and aft ends

The shape of the stern is described by a series of decreasing circular arcs (perpendicular to the curving keel/stermpost) defined in drawing 26. This drawing was used to create the small frame templates shown below:

![P7161524.JPG](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_05/P7161524.thumb.JPG.2a9b82a9918aff034ba1885a93fabf60.JPG)

The planks were steamed and bent over these templates clamps and a variety of elastic bands and wooden battens as shown below:

![P7161523.JPG](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_05/P7161523.thumb.JPG.f5d2c8335e7c35e558a7279cb7614896.JPG)

## Post 30 — POST 24 STERN FAIRING  (May 29 May 29)

As a short holiday from canopy planking I turned to the stern fairing piece.

I started with a piece of wire to get an approximate shape that looked about right and then made up an (again approximate) pattern in balsawood. The vertical timber is loose, held in place with the steel square (set on a piece of balsa sheet resting on the aft platform) to confirm the fit to the first stanchion.

As you can see in the following image work on the stern fairing is combined with trimming the aft end of the quarterdeck planking.

![PA072715-small.jpg.41a0fa1c6194ba0fa0e92c8672991f40.jpg](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_05/PA072715-small.jpg.41a0fa1c6194ba0fa0e92c8672991f40.thumb.jpg.a26154e4e00d63b70de9e75aba429db4.jpg)

## Post 39 — POST 31 MANIKINS  (June 1 Jun 1)


June 1 Jun 1

I had been looking for a scale wooden artists manikin to demonstrate ergonomics of rowing equipment geometry etc. but they are all on much too large a scale for my model. So, I decided to have a go myself at a 1:24 version.

For the dimensions I found a good source of ergonomic data for US Marines ([https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA581918.pdf](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA581918.pdf)). I took the data for the 50th percentile marine and scaled it according to the mean height of a classical Greek male (approx. 5 ' 7" as identified from Archaeological studies of skeletal remains ref S. C. Bisel, J. L. Angel, "Health and Nutrition in Mycenean Greece: A Study in Human Skeletal Remains," in N. Wilkie, W.D.E. Coulson (eds.) Contributions to Aegean Archaeology: Studies in honor of William A. MacDonald (Minneapolis 1985)).

Given both US Marines and Athenian oarsmen are likely to be of a similar muscular build, I thought this approach would give a reasonable approximation. I know that trials on the full size of Olympias had some difficulties fitting 20th century oarsmen in some of the rowing positions...

Anyway, the resulting model is shown below:

![Athenian marine 1.jpg](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_06/Athenianmarine1.thumb.jpg.5412ca283b04d239f4cc2ee0012b5bc8.jpg)

I've used two sort of joints to give the required mobility:

1. ball ended pins to simulate the ball and socket joints of the shoulder and hip (and the rotational joints of the neck and heels)

2. simple one degree of freedom pin joints for the knees and elbows.

This works for an oarsman at around midstroke as shown below:

![Athenian Marine 2.jpg](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_06/AthenianMarine2.thumb.jpg.f9236e7a8524d7ba6178ca9e5eb83a5c.jpg)

And finally standing on the helmsman's platform of the model to give an indication of scale:

![Athenian Marine 3.jpg](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_06/AthenianMarine3.thumb.jpg.87fefb92d52ad6c99521ca0371bd1ffd.jpg)

Later I modified the elbow and hip joints to extend the reach of the rowing stroke. An "as fitted" drawing including these changes shown below:

![Manikin.jpeg](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_06/Manikin.thumb.jpeg.30b885097de95e67f9365d7b532f8e6b.jpeg)

More information can be found in the following shop note: R Braithwaite, _Athenian Manikins at 1:24 scale_, Nautical Research Journal, Vol 70 No.4, p 374, 2025

## Post 51 — POST 41 COATING  (June 6 Jun 6)

June 6 Jun 6

I spring 2024 I finally got the courage to coat the model as I was concerned that it might start to discolour and stain with age and handling.

I chose polyurethane varnish mainly to give a waterproof finish in case it ever does go in the water…(which is why I've been using epoxy throughout as an adhesive). I also applied the same finish to a model I built 40 years ago and like the way it has aged…

In order to assess the impact of 20 years of aging on the bare wood I took a trial panel of stiffened planning I prepared at the beginning of the project and made up a brand new one. I then treated them both as described below and the results were as follows (the panel on the right is the old one)

![P3190273.JPG](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_06/P3190273.thumb.JPG.96a38b2cc64b0dda585acec78b9485b5.JPG)

Reassured by this experiment I started work on the model…

I dismantled it completely, hoovered all the dust out and cleaned it as well as I could with white spirit, after sanding everything I could get at with 600 grit paper.

Doing all this required a fourth evolution of my build cradle...

The upper works are very light which makes inverting the model interesting. I’ve adapted the cradle so it supports the model inverted on two beams which pass from port to starboard beside the upper thole pins. This way the weight is taken on the lower outrigger which is supported by numerous brackets and so should be strong enough to take any forces I subjected it to while sanding and varnishing the hull.

![P3180270.JPG](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_06/P3180270.thumb.JPG.dbf15e8574606fe17e0afa7d0193e74e.JPG)

After sanding, I applied 5 coats Ronseal Polyurethane matt applied by brush. 1 st coat 40% White Spirit and 25% thereafter, sanding with 1500 grit between coats on planked areas and other visible/ accessible areas.

Removed most Thranite thole pins prior to varnishing and took great care to avoid varnishing into the small wooden blocks holding the captive nuts for assembly.

Pleased to say, none of my fears materialized:

1. The older work didn’t look any different from the fresh stuff.

2. I managed to avoid blocking up the threads of all the captive nuts

3. The lightweight structure didn’t distort.

Here is a composite image of all the main components after sealing (The canopy units are numbered 1 to 6 for the stern port (P) and starboard (S)):

## Post 60 — POST 49 RIG DESIGN  (June 15 Jun 15)


June 15 Jun 15

There were a number of collaborators in the design and construction of Olympias and O.T.P Roberts was responsible for the design of the rig (I have two articles by him describing the design and subsequent sailing trials, published in, The Trireme Project, Operational Experience 1987-90 Lessons Learnt and The Trireme Trials 1988, both published by Oxbow books). The rig design featured a large and a small sail and a large and small yard as described in Naval Inventories at the time, although there is apparently no iconographic evidence for a two masted rig in galleys of the last millennium BC. Morrison and Williams (Greek Oared Warships 900-322BC, Cambridge 1968) point to evidence that the main mast was unrigged for battle and the smaller "boat sail" was retained to be used for downwind escape if necessary.

The forward mast on Olympias also serves as a useful gantry to raise the much heavier main mast (method described in drawing MSR 13)

The sails on Olympias were rigged on a yard hoisted by twin halyards but is unsupported by lifts. As mentioned before the mast is only supported by fore and backstays and has no shrouds (as designed) requiring a substantial tabernacle.

Pictures of Olympias, however, clearly show shrouds fitted but Roberts states that these were fitted because “Captain Dimitris Papadas…..took fright when seeing the tabernacles into which the masts were to be stepped and insisted that shrouds be fitted”. “Unfortunately, he fitted shrouds as in a fore and aft rig which made it impossible to brace round a reefed square sail”.

The sail is controlled by brails and the downward pressure of these (and the yard braces) is thought to account for the downward curving yards seen in the iconography. An illustration of this for a one masted galley is shown below.(figure taken from The Sailing Rig of Olympias, p30, The Trireme Project, Oxbow Monograph 31):

![P9100142.JPG](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_06/P9100142.thumb.JPG.2398fa539d6e6b45c30fc13739638b14.JPG)

There are no reef points, as such on the sails. Sail reduction, and furling, is accomplished by brails passing through rings on the leeward side of the sails, and fixed to the foot of the sail. Fore and aft balancing of the rig could be achieved by partly brailing the sail to move the center of effort fore and aft to counter weather or lee helm as required, as shown below (figure also taken from Roberts article in The Trireme Project.) Trials on Olympias also showed that slightly drawing up the middle brails when sailing downwind. Apparently, this helps maintain the rectangular shape of the sail, preventing the clews pulling in and spilling the wind out of the bottom of the sail. In the two masted configuration this also helps the airflow over the forward boat sail.

![P9100141.JPG](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_06/P9100141.thumb.JPG.fa187ff5fb89e480396a6e629d844394.JPG)

Performance on trial suggested that progress could be made to windward with close reaching up to 60 degrees off the wind while making 7 degrees of leeway. In light weather a sailing speed of 2.5 kts could be doubled by light rowing as a bireme (i.e. two top rows of oars only) which would have enabled 1/3 of the crew to rest on passage, even in light winds.

Roberts comments that the main yard on the reconstruction was over engineered and so did not flex in the way suggested by the above illustrations. It was also very heavy, so the original plan to make the halliards from 45mm diameter leather were amended to use 20mm 3 strand polyester rope for safety reasons...

I decided to model the rig as designed by Roberts, rather than as built (it is also a lot easier for me to omit shrouds into my disassembling design…).

Roberts design for the rig is documented by drawings MSR 1-10.

## Post 63 — POST 51 MAST HEAD FITTINGS (PT 1)  (June 18 Jun 18)


June 18 Jun 18

Starting with the masts drawing MSR 2 states that the head of the mast should be “tapered to fit the truck casting”, so it makes sense to make the truck casting before the mast…

Twin Halyards pass through the mast truck to hoist the main yard. In Roberts design these trucks are bronze castings that include fairleads for these halyards and a shoulder above them to support the forestay and backstay. The castings for both main and foremast are defined by drawing MSR 7.

## Post 72 — POST 60 OARS (PART 1)  (June 25 Jun 25)


June 25 Jun 25

I must admit I found 170 oars quite daunting (why didn't I build a skull, or even a four instead...).

The efficiency of any oar system is critically dependent on the weight and inertia of the oars. The lower these two numbers are the less energy is wasted moving the oars backwards and forwards, maximizing the proportion of the oarsman’s energy that is transmitted into the water.

In 350 BC Theophrastus wrote that silver fir possesses many layers, like an onion. He went on to say that to produce a strong oar-spar, craftsmen had to meticulously strip these layers away one by one (Theophrastus, Enquiry into Plants, 350BC). This is a similar technique to producing a longbow where the back of the bow is produced in a similar manner to make sure that the wood fibers are continuous at the point of maximum tension. Both the Trireme and the Longbow are good examples of where military needs have driven people to develop sophisticated weapons that derive the maximum performance from the materials available.

In the case of Olympias, the design of the oars evolved during the life of the vessel. The original design, and the evidence on which it is based, is described in John Coates book, _The Athenian Trireme_. The Athenian inventories give two lengths of oar carried on Triremes at 9 and 91/2 cubits. The length of cubit assumed for the Olympias design was 444mm so these come out at 4.2 and 4.0m long with the shorter oars being utilized at the ends of the vessel where the narrowing form restricted room.

Original trials of the oar system (prior to the build of Olympias) involved a section of the boat that was taken to various events including the London Boat Show and Henley Regatta to demonstrate feasibility. The oars used on this were naval pattern with 1500x150mm blades. These trials suggested that different blade shapes should be adopted depending on the angle of the oar to the waterplane.

The original oars developed for Olympias incorporated simpler spade shaped blades spliced into the shaft, (rather like those on the 5th red figure vase from the British Museum - see below) for the upper two levels and a much narrower blade for the lower level oars.

![Siren_Painter_ARV_289_1_Odysseus_and_the_Sirens_-_three_erotes_(04).jpg](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_06/Siren_Painter_ARV_289_1_Odysseus_and_the_Sirens_-_three_erotes_(04)

These oars were developed as a result of Sea trials as described in The Trireme Trials 1988.

The original oars were made from Douglas Fir (sawn rather than carefully whittled from saplings as per Theophrastus!) and weighed over 12 kg, which increased to 17 kg once a lead counterweight was added inboard to improve “weight in hand”. The high moment of inertia of these oars meant that a considerable amount of energy was expended on reversing the direction of the oars during the stroke and a lighter design was developed made from a lighter wood (Sitka Spruce) and with a common teardrop shaped blade for all levels.

These new oars proved more effective, but funding precluded building a full set and a compromise was reached that involved butchering the existing blades to reduce their weight and modify the blade shape.

I decided to go with the original design, in line with my intention to reflect the vessel on its first sea trials and also so as the model represents a coherent state of the design.

There only full drawing of the original oars is Plan 15 which shows the Thalmian oars. Plan 15 d shows the new spruce oar design and plan 15e shows how the oars were modified after the 1988 trials.

There is a sketch showing the original oars but it is un-dimensioned and doesn’t show how the blades are shaped.

However, I produced the following sketches to reconstruct the design of the original oars based on scaling from this sketch and the information on the Plan 15x drawings.

## Post 79 — POST 66 RAM (PART 1)  (July 6 Jul 6)


July 6 Jul 6

Sadly, despite thousands of warships being built and the numerous naval battles fought in the Mediterranean in classical times no ramming warships have ever been discovered intact.

However, several rams have been found and taken together with iconographic evidence (pottery etc., a reasonable reconstruction of the development of this technology can be made ( S DeCasien, Proto-Rams: Peicing together the Early History of Naval Ram Development, The Ancient Near East Today, Voll. 11.5, May 2023).

DeCasien divides ram development into the following phases:

• Early Greek Geometric Period (900-700BC) iconographic evidence shows keel projections extending past the stem. These might have simply been to improve hydrodynamic efficiency.

• Late Geometric Period: Bronze sheathed rams such as the conical ram shown on the Kouyunjik Palace relief.

• 600 BC The development of an effective ram in the form of blunt boar head rams shown in various Greek vases. These would have been less likely to become lodged in and enemy vessel and would have cased more extensive damage than the earlier conical sheathed rams.

• 500BC development of mortice and tennon joinery enabled a stronger hull which was combined with three bladed rams to enable a really effective ramming weapon which was also more hydrodynamically efficient than the blunt boar head rams.

A number of examples (sadly much later than the approx. 480 BC time of Olympias) have been found of this latter type.

The most dramatic example is the Athlit Ram, thought to have belonged to a large roman galley. It was discovered by the late Yehoshua Ramon in 1980, in the northern bay of Athlit. The Athlit ram is one of the largest single bronze castings ever found. The total weight of the bronze casting is 465 kg and it is now thought to have been cast by the lost-wax process,

![Athlit_Ram_Haifa_090912_04.jpg](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_07/Athlit_Ram_Haifa_090912_04.thumb.jpg.b84ddbca9ffc139132f349a5b108a5ce.jpg)

More recently (some time after Olympias was designed) a number of smaller rams were discovered off the coast of Sicily thought date to the battle of the Egadi Islands in 241 BC and to have been fitted to Carthaginian and Roman ships of approximately 25 to 28m in length (S. Tusa J Royal, The landscape of the naval battle at the Egadi Islands (241BC), Journal of Roman Archaeology, 2012)

John coated chose to base his ram on the Athlit Ram, reducing its weight from 400 to 200 kg to reflect the smaller size of Olympias.

So, there are a number of alternatives I considered for my model.

1. I could fabricate if from thin brass plate and, hopefully manage to replicate the actual construction of the ram on Olympias and even have some hope of achieving the scale weight (approx. 17 grams)

2. I could follow the lost wax casting approach believed to have been used on the Athlit ram.

After some research, I excluded lost wax casting because the minimum wall thickness achievable is (apparently) 0.6mm which would be much too thick and overweight.

There is no manufacturing drawing listed for the Olympias ram, although some information is given on Plan No 20 and there are photographs of Olympias such as the one below which show some of the welds:

![ram.jpg](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_07/ram.jpg.2c56976c64f6b23597e1e58b5ed4b0ce.jpg)

The upper part of the ram sheathing can be seen to be made up of a number of pieces, but welded together. I thought this would be difficult with 0.2 mm brass sheet (which was required to meet the scale weight) so looked into shaping this part from a single piece.

The following image shows the pattern I made up out of brass and wood for shaping the upper part of the Ram sheathing, alongside a test piece from 0.1mm copper that I managed to shape without any annealing required.

![PA061311.JPG](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_07/PA061311.thumb.JPG.a5f6faf30a3d411fd666d477e26e89c1.JPG)

It proved impossible to form 0.2 mm brass into this shape by simply bashing it.

So, I tried heating it to red hot in a coal fire and then quenching, but found that too difficult to control and ended up melting the sheet…

I already had a small blowtorch, which was clearly inadequate for this so purchased the cheap larger one shown below:

![PA081312.JPG](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_07/PA081312.thumb.JPG.965ece4a8c2c3e312585d2060610c63c.JPG)

## Post 80 — POST 67 RAM (PART 2)  (July 7 Jul 7)


July 7 Jul 7

After a few practices I found I was able to shape the piece with about 30 cycles of:

1. Shaping over the pattern using a brass hammer.

2. Heating to a uniform dull red with the blowtorch

3. Quenching in cold water.

The (untrimmed) result is shown below:

![PA081313.JPG](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_07/PA081313.thumb.JPG.e9769152ad790f8dac9e1acfd8c7a1d0.JPG)

The plates of the ram were made from 1mm plate and I considered the following options for joining them:

Using gold solder of various melting points to try to match the color with the brass

Using silver solder of various melting points with a potential color match problem.

Kurt suggested to me using a much softer (lower melting point) lead free silver content solder called Stay-Brite as used by Ken Foran in his amazing brass models (K. Foran, Model Building with Brass, 2nd Edition, Schiffer 2018).

After trying gold and silver solder on some test joint configurations, equivalent to what was required on the ram, I went for silver solder. The colour difference on the joint seemed insignificant and likely to disappear with age and I could manage the long fillet joints that I was concerned about. I didn’t get round to trying Stay-Brite, which would probably have been easier (albeit with a slightly weaker joint?).

I used tubes of silver solder paste in 4 different melting point solders for building the ram so that new joints could be made without having to heat to temperatures that might melt a previous joint:

Hard 745-778 degrees centigrade

Medium 720-765 degrees

Easy 705 -723 degrees

Extra Easy 667-709 degrees

In addition, a Thermogel paste was used thickly on areas of the ram where I didn’t want heat to go.

![P6302577.JPG](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_07/P6302577.thumb.JPG.8ee652e297aab0b4b55530c669dfff3b.JPG)

I did find that when soldering 1mm plate to 0.2mm plate the amount of heat required to get the 1mm plate up to temperature risked moving and distorting the 0.2mm plate. So invested some effort in devising jigs that firmly held the joint in place (without the jig ending up soldered to the ram…)

The first soldering operation was joining the upper sheathing to the upper ram plate.

The process is shown in the series of images below:

The first image shows the parts to be joined together with the jig/clamping arrangement used to hold them in alignment.

The second image shows the piece just after soldering (using a “Hard” silver solder paste) with the jig still connected and the final image shows it after removal from the jig, pickling in “picklean” and some light sanding. In this image you can see some coppery patches that apparently occur due to evaporation of some of the zinc on the surface of the brass alloy during soldering.

All the ram plates were made oversize (width and length) to allow for final finishing after all the soldering operations were complete.

![PC022109a.jpg](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_07/PC022109a.thumb.jpg.3929502614409e761929da6dc0a7d011.jpg)

The base of the same jig used to form the top sheathing was shaped to the curve of the bottom sheathing and was used to form it. There is much less double curvature in this piece than the top sheathing and so this was accomplished quite easily.

Then it was a case of shaping the bottom ram plate and soldering it to the bottom sheathing. The images below show the relevant parts and a test fit up of the clamping/jig arrangement prior to applying the (Hard) silver solder paste:

![PC032117a.jpg](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_07/PC032117a.thumb.jpg.c83dea70c5bd678afe89a640376360c1.jpg)

## Post 83 — POST 70 ANCHORS  (July 12 Jul 12)


July 12 Jul 12

Many of the details on Olympias are not reconstructions of Athenian practice but included for safety or expediency reasons. The anchors are a good example of this. The anchors carries are of a modern fisherman type (approximately 35kg scaled from photos!).

It is believed that 2 anchors were carried in the 6th Cent BC (As Olympias) earlier stone anchors having been phased out in favor of wooden hooks with lead cored stocks (although 4 examples are listed with iron anchors (Shear, Julia L. "Fragments of Naval Inventories from the Athenian Agora." Hesperia, vol. 64, no. 2, 1995, pp. 179–224. JSTOR, www.jstor.org/stable/148054.)

Olympias was moored “Mediterranean Style” stern on to a convenient beach. One or two bow anchors were dropped and like paid out as the vessel is rowed astern until the keel grounds on the beach, at which point a couple of stern lines were taken ashore and secured to something solid (e.g. to trees).

There are no drawings of the anchors on Olympias. A sketch reconstructed from a photograph is shown below:

![Anchor-Design.jpg](https://media.invisioncic.com/j336806/monthly_2026_07/Anchor-Design.thumb.jpg.fe9d922b679d558373b14e8730ec93b8.jpg)

I calculated that this anchor would weigh approximately 35 kg if made from iron.

The anchor ropes are not defined in Olympias’s rigging list but look roughly 24mm in diameter…
